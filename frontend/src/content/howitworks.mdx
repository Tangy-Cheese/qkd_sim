Quantum key distribution (QKD) lets two people, always called **Alice** and **Bob**, agree on a
secret key using single photons. The key is then used to encrypt messages with an ordinary cipher.
The trick is not that the photons are hard to intercept, it is that **measuring a quantum state
disturbs it**. An eavesdropper, **Eve**, cannot copy an unknown photon (the no-cloning theorem) and
cannot look at it without risking changing it, so Alice and Bob can check afterwards whether anyone
was listening.

<Window title="1. BB84 - prepare and measure">

Alice sends single photons polarised in one of two **bases**:

| Basis | bit 0 | bit 1 |
|---|---|---|
| Z (rectilinear) | $\lvert 0 \rangle$, horizontal | $\lvert 1 \rangle$, vertical |
| X (diagonal) | $\lvert + \rangle$, 45° | $\lvert - \rangle$, 135° |

where $\lvert \pm \rangle = (\lvert 0 \rangle \pm \lvert 1 \rangle)/\sqrt{2}$. For every photon Alice picks
a random bit and a random basis. Bob measures each photon in his own random basis:

- **Same basis**: Bob gets Alice's bit.
- **Different basis**: Bob gets a coin flip, since $\lvert \langle 0 \vert + \rangle \rvert^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

Afterwards they announce their bases over a public channel (never the bits) and throw away every
photon where the bases differ. This is **sifting**, and it keeps about half of the photons that arrive.

**Catching Eve.** Eve measures each photon in a random basis and sends Bob a new one in the state she
saw. Half the time she picks the wrong basis and sends the wrong kind of photon, and then Bob's
result is wrong half the time even when his basis matches Alice's. So Eve causes errors in

$$
Q_{\text{Eve}} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = 25\%
$$

of the sifted key. Alice and Bob compare a sample of their bits, see the error rate, and know someone
was listening.

</Window>

<Window title="2. E91 - entanglement and the Bell test">

In E91 nobody prepares the key. A source makes pairs of photons in the entangled singlet state

$$
\lvert \Psi^- \rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( \lvert H V \rangle - \lvert V H \rangle \right)
$$

and sends one photon of each pair to Alice and one to Bob. Neither photon has a polarisation of its
own until it is measured, but the pair always gives **opposite** results along the same axis. If Alice
and Bob measure with polarisers at angles $a$ and $b$:

$$
P(\text{opposite}) = \cos^2(a - b)
$$

$$
E(a, b) = P(\text{same}) - P(\text{opposite}) = -\cos 2(a - b)
$$

Try it, at $0^\circ$ the results are always opposite and at $45^\circ$ they are completely random:

<AngleDemo />

**Angles.** Alice picks randomly from $0^\circ, 22.5^\circ, 45^\circ$ and Bob from $22.5^\circ, 45^\circ, 67.5^\circ$. After
measuring, they announce their angles:

- **Same angle** ($22.5^\circ$ or $45^\circ$ on both sides, 2 of the 9 combinations): perfectly opposite results.
  Bob flips his bits and these become the **sifted key**.
- **CHSH angles** (4 of the 9 combinations): they announce these results and use them for the Bell test.
- **The rest** (3 of the 9) are thrown away.

**The Bell test.** From the four CHSH angle pairs they work out

$$
S = E(0^\circ, 22.5^\circ) - E(0^\circ, 67.5^\circ) + E(45^\circ, 22.5^\circ) + E(45^\circ, 67.5^\circ)
$$

Any classical source of correlations, where each photon carries a fixed answer for every angle,
obeys the CHSH inequality $\lvert S \rvert \le 2$. Entangled photons break it, each correlation is
$\mp 1/\sqrt{2}$ and

$$
\lvert S \rvert = 4 \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83
$$

<BellMeter S={2.83} classical={2} quantum={2.83} />

**Catching Eve.** If Eve measures Bob's photon, the entanglement is gone. Alice and Bob are left with
photons that each have a definite polarisation, which is exactly the classical case, so
$\lvert S \rvert \le 2$. In this simulator Eve's interception drops it to about $1.4$ and the run is
aborted:

<BellMeter S={1.4} classical={2} quantum={2.83} />

The nice thing about E91 is that the security check does not depend on trusting the source. Even if
Eve built the source herself, a Bell value close to $2\sqrt{2}$ proves the photons were entangled
and so nobody else can know the results.

</Window>

<Window title="3. From sifted key to secret key">

Even without Eve the sifted keys of Alice and Bob are not quite equal. The **quantum bit error rate**
(QBER) is the fraction of sifted bits that disagree:

$$
Q = \frac{\text{bits that differ}}{\text{sifted key length}}
$$

Two classical steps turn the sifted key into a secret key:

1. **Error correction.** Alice and Bob compare parities of blocks of their keys over the public channel
   and fix the bits that differ (the Cascade protocol). Every parity they announce leaks a bit to
   Eve. The ideal leak is $h(Q)$ bits per key bit, real protocols leak $f_{\text{EC}} \, h(Q)$ with
   $f_{\text{EC}} \approx 1.15$.
2. **Privacy amplification.** Eve may also know up to $h(Q)$ bits per key bit from the photons she
   disturbed. Hashing the key down to a shorter one removes her knowledge.

Here $h$ is the binary entropy

$$
h(Q) = -Q \log_2 Q - (1 - Q) \log_2 (1 - Q)
$$

so the fraction of the sifted key that survives is (Shor and Preskill)

$$
r = 1 - f_{\text{EC}} \, h(Q) - h(Q)
$$

and the **secure key rate**, in secret bits per photon sent, is

$$
R = \frac{\text{sifted key length}}{\text{photons sent}} \times r
$$

When $Q$ climbs above about $10\%$, $r$ hits zero: Eve could know as much as Bob, nothing secret
is left and the run is **aborted**. Eve's $25\%$ in BB84 is well past that line.

</Window>

<Window title="4. Fibre loss and dark counts">

Photons get absorbed in the fibre. Loss is measured in decibels, about $\alpha = 0.2$ dB/km for
telecom fibre, so after $L$ km a photon survives with probability

$$
\eta = 10^{-\alpha L / 10}
$$

At 100 km that is $10^{-2}$: only one photon in a hundred arrives.

Bob's detectors also click now and then with no photon at all, a **dark count**, with probability
$p_{\text{dc}}$ (here $10^{-3}$) per time slot. A dark count gives a random bit, wrong half the time. Close
by, real photons drown them out. Far away, real photons become rare and the dark counts take over:

$$
Q_{\text{dark}} \approx \frac{p_{\text{dc}} / 2}{\eta + p_{\text{dc}}}
$$

Setting this to the $10\%$ limit gives $\eta = 4 \, p_{\text{dc}}$, around 24 dB or **120 km** of fibre.
Beyond that no key can be made, however many photons are sent. Gobby, Yuan and Shields reached
122 km in a real experiment in 2004.

</Window>

<Window title="5. Networks of trusted nodes">

Since a single link stops working after about 100 km, longer distances need a chain of
**trusted nodes**. Each neighbouring pair of nodes runs BB84 over their own fibre and gets its own key.

**Choosing a route.** The whole route is only as good as its worst link, because every link must
produce a key. The simulator uses a modified Dijkstra's algorithm that finds the route whose
**worst** link has the lowest loss, and breaks ties with the lowest total loss.

**Relaying the key.** Say the route is Alice, Charlie, Bob, with link keys $k_{AC}$ and $k_{CB}$.
Alice's key is $K = k_{AC}$, which Charlie also holds. Charlie announces

$$
K \oplus k_{CB}
$$

publicly. Bob knows $k_{CB}$ and gets $K = (K \oplus k_{CB}) \oplus k_{CB}$. To anyone else the
announcement is a one-time pad and looks like random noise. Longer routes repeat this hop by hop,
and the final key is as long as the shortest link key.

The catch is in the name: every trusted node sees $K$ in the clear, so they have to be trusted.
Real networks like SECOQC in Vienna and the 4,600 km Chinese network work this way.

</Window>
